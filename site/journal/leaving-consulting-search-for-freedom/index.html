Freedom & Entrepreneurship

Why I Left Consulting to Explore a Different Kind of Freedom

I moved from consulting to long-term travel, and later returned to professional life. The real lesson wasn't that one path was right—it was learning to choose.

By Serge Abi · 29 October 2026 · 6 min read

For years, my professional life had a recognizable shape. I had studied business and consulting, built a career in France and learned how to navigate organizations, projects and expectations. From 2014 to 2018, I worked in consulting roles where structure, deadlines and performance were part of everyday life.

Then, in 2018, I began another kind of education: an extended period of travel and self-exploration that took me from California to Bali. I stepped away from the familiar shape of my professional life to experience a different rhythm.

This isn't a story about consulting being wrong or travel being right. It is about asking what freedom means when a conventional path no longer feels like the only possibility.

What consulting gave me

It would be too easy to dismiss my earlier years as a mistake. Consulting developed skills I still use: understanding complex problems, working with different people, identifying priorities and turning ideas into action.

Professional experience can be a foundation rather than a cage. Even when you decide to change direction, the things you've learned don't disappear. They can travel with you.

One question helped frame the transition: Do I want to leave this work—or do I want more freedom in the way I live? Those aren't always the same question.

From California to Bali: a different classroom

Travel changed the environment in which I was asking questions. Instead of organizing life around a familiar office routine, I spent time in new places, encountering other ways to work, think and relate to the world. It was also a period in which I explored philosophical teachings and spiritual practices.

Changing location can offer perspective, but geography isn't a shortcut to self-understanding. Old fears, expectations and habits can follow us. A different landscape may make them easier to notice; it doesn't automatically resolve them.

For me, the more interesting question gradually became: Can freedom be something I practice, not simply somewhere I go?

My path wasn't a straight line out of corporate life

There is a popular version of this story: leave the office, never return, and live happily ever after. My experience was more layered. After my nomadic chapter, I also returned to consulting work. That matters because life transitions rarely follow a perfect narrative arc.

Returning to familiar work doesn't erase an earlier exploration. Starting a business doesn't guarantee autonomy. Living abroad doesn't automatically create peace. Every arrangement brings its own trade-offs.

With time, my focus expanded toward entrepreneurship, inner work and creating projects that connect practical independence with a more deliberate way of living.

The six kinds of freedom worth considering

When people imagine leaving a corporate career, location freedom tends to dominate the conversation. But it's only one dimension:

  • Time freedom: having influence over your schedule, including time for the people and practices that matter.
  • Location freedom: being able to choose where you live or work, within real-world constraints.
  • Financial freedom: enough stability and choice to avoid being driven entirely by immediate pressure.
  • Creative freedom: room to build, experiment and express ideas.
  • Relational freedom: relationships in which you can be honest about who you are becoming.
  • Inner freedom: noticing when fear, habit or the desire for approval is making the choice for you.

You can gain one kind of freedom while losing another. Independent work may offer flexible hours but increase uncertainty. A stable job may reduce location flexibility while supporting the family life you want. The point is not to rank lifestyles; it is to know which trade-offs you are consciously choosing.

Before leaving your job, ask these questions

Not everyone needs to resign, launch a startup or buy a one-way ticket. If you feel drawn to a different life, test the idea with the same seriousness you would give an important business decision.

  1. What exactly am I trying to change? Is it the work, the team, the pace, the location, the lack of meaning or something deeper?
  2. What am I grateful for in my current path? Preserve the assets you can carry forward: experience, relationships, savings and skills.
  3. What would a small trial look like? Test a freelance project, make a financial plan, take leave, or reshape responsibilities before making an irreversible choice.
  4. What are the costs? Consider income, legal obligations, health coverage, relationships, energy and the unexpected work of running your own venture.
  5. Who am I hoping to become? Try to answer this without using a job title or a location.

What I carry forward

My path has continued through entrepreneurship, personal exploration and work with people facing their own crossroads. I don't think freedom is a final destination where uncertainty ends. It is an ongoing relationship with choice, responsibility and attention.

The consulting chapter taught me how to build and execute. Travel widened the questions I was willing to ask. Neither chapter needs to cancel the other.

Freedom is not always leaving the life you have. Sometimes it is learning how to participate in it by choice.
